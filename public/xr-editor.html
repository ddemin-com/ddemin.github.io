Overview

About the Project

XR Editor is a web-based surgical planning platform designed to replace the fragmented, multi-tool workflow that orthopedic and spinal surgeons currently rely on before entering the operating room. The product allows surgeons and their teams to load DICOM imaging data, reconstruct 3D anatomical models, annotate and edit them directly in-browser, and then review those models in augmented reality via a HoloLens headset — all without switching applications or converting files.

The project ran over 14 months and involved close collaboration with practicing surgeons, radiologists, and biomedical engineers. My responsibility spanned the entire UX lifecycle: from initial discovery and domain research, through information architecture and interaction design, to high-fidelity prototyping and facilitated usability testing with clinical users.

The platform was built for a startup in the medical device space. It is currently in active clinical evaluation and has been piloted in two hospital networks across Europe.

Context

Market Research

⚒

Fragmented Tooling

Surgical planning across the market relies on 3–6 separate applications that don't communicate. Surgeons move between PACS viewers, segmentation software, 3D modelers, file converters, and AR headset loaders — often manually re-exporting at each step.

🖥

Desktop Lock-in

Existing solutions (Materialise, Brainlab, Stryker Virtual Planning) require expensive licensed desktop software installed on specific hospital machines. Remote review and team collaboration are either unavailable or require proprietary hardware ecosystems.

👥

Zero Collaboration

Pre-operative planning is almost entirely asynchronous and solo. There is no real-time collaboration model in current tools — surgical teams share screenshots via email or hold in-person review sessions around a single monitor.

The market had conflated "feature completeness" with usability. Products were clinically capable but operationally burdensome. Our opportunity wasn't to build better features — it was to build a fundamentally more coherent experience.

Decision Making

Trade-offs

Rejected

Desktop-native 3D viewer

Maximum rendering performance, familiar paradigm for surgeons used to OsiriX/Horos. Full GPU access, offline capable.

→
Our Choice

Web-based 3D environment (WebGL)

Zero install friction, cross-device access, real-time collaboration potential, and remote review without VPN or file sharing.

Reasoning: Clinical teams are distributed. The ability for a radiologist, surgeon, and assistant to review the same model simultaneously outweighs the performance delta achievable in WebGL vs. native, especially at the model complexity we were targeting.

Rejected

Full CAD-style scene hierarchy

Maximum flexibility for power users. Every object, layer, and modifier exposed in a tree. Familiar to 3D artists and engineers.

→
Our Choice

Anatomy-first flat structure

Objects organized by anatomical region, not technical hierarchy. Surgeons think in anatomy, not scene graphs.

Reasoning: Every surgeon we tested with described their mental model in anatomical terms ("the femoral head," "L4-L5 segment"). Imposing a technical hierarchy created cognitive friction. Flattening to anatomy reduced navigation errors by 60% in early testing.

Rejected

Auto-save with version history

Standard SaaS pattern. Familiar, low-friction. Hides save complexity from the user. Common in Figma, Notion, etc.

→
Our Choice

Explicit save with named checkpoints

Surgeons must consciously approve a planning state before it can be used in AR review. Named checkpoints match clinical documentation norms.

Reasoning: In a clinical context, the version of a surgical plan that gets loaded into the OR must be intentional. Auto-save in a regulated environment creates traceability problems. Explicit save is a feature, not a limitation.

Research

Research & Discovery

We ran a focused discovery sprint over 6 weeks at the start of the project. The goal was not to validate assumptions — we started with very few — but to build a grounded understanding of how surgical planning actually happens, what mental models surgeons use, and where the friction concentrates. We conducted contextual inquiry sessions in two hospitals, shadowing surgeons during pre-operative planning sessions and debriefing with them immediately afterward.

Core Insight

Surgeons don't think about their tools — they think about the anatomy. Any interface that forces them to think about the software is already a failure. The best interaction is the one that disappears.

01

Time is surgical currency

Every minute saved in planning is reinvested in patient time. Speed isn't a luxury — it's part of clinical quality.

02

Verification is non-negotiable

Surgeons need to confirm that what they see in planning matches what they will encounter in surgery. Spatial verification is not a feature — it's trust.

03

Context collapse at handoff

When a plan is exported and sent to a scrub tech or assistant, critical context — reasoning, annotations, orientation — is lost. The plan arrives as a file, not as knowledge.

Strategy

Design Strategy

Crisis Moment

At week 8, after the first round of low-fi testing, we discovered that surgeons found the 3D editor interface — despite being well-organized — still too far removed from how they think about anatomy. A participant said: "I understand what all these controls do, but I don't understand why I'm touching controls at all. I just want to move this bone." This forced us to rebuild the interaction model from the object-control layer upward.

The crisis moment reoriented our entire design strategy. Instead of designing an editor and layering anatomy concepts on top, we designed from anatomy outward — the objects in the scene are anatomical structures first, and editable objects second. The controls surface only when contextually relevant to the selected anatomy. This required close collaboration with the 3D engineers to build a context-aware tool system.

Before: Tool-centric model

1.
Open tool panel → select a tool mode → then interact with anatomy
2.

3.

4.

After: Anatomy-centric model

1.

2.
Tool panel adapts to selection — irrelevant controls hidden
3.
Primary verbs are anatomical: position, orient, measure, annotate
4.
Scene hierarchy uses clinical labels — Femoral Head, L4 Vertebra, Implant

5 Key UX Decisions

Decision 01

Anatomy-first scene hierarchy

Structure the scene panel around anatomical regions (spine segment, joint, implant) rather than 3D object types. Labels use clinical terminology sourced directly from surgeon vocabulary captured during research sessions.

Scene Hierarchy — Anatomy-First Structure
Decision 02

Context-sensitive tool surfacing

Tools appear only when relevant to the currently selected object and editing state. A bone segment selected in "review" mode shows measurement tools. The same segment in "edit" mode shows position and orientation controls. No mode-switching UI required.

Context-Sensitive Tool Panel — State Comparison
Decision 03

Named checkpoint save model

Replace auto-save with explicit named checkpoints that require a brief label ("Pre-op positioning," "Final implant placement"). Each checkpoint is timestamped and attributed. Only named checkpoints can be sent to XR Review — preventing accidental review of interim states.

Checkpoint Save Dialog — Naming & Review Flow
Decision 04

Single-action XR sync

Sending a plan to the HoloLens is a single button action. All format conversion, optimization, and transmission happens server-side. The user sees a status indicator and receives confirmation when the headset is loaded and ready. No file management required.

XR Sync — One-Click to HoloLens Flow
Decision 05

Shared plan with role-differentiated access

All team members can view and annotate the same plan simultaneously. Surgeons have edit rights. Assistants and residents have comment-and-query rights. Annotations are attributed and persistent — surviving the XR handoff into the AR view on the headset.

Measurement Methodology

Timing data was captured by a facilitator with a stopwatch during moderated usability sessions. Participants were given identical planning tasks (locate case, review imaging, position implant, send to AR) and completed them first in their existing workflow, then in XR Editor. Participants were blinded to which metrics we were capturing. Qualitative data was coded from session recordings and debrief interviews.

Reflection

Key Learnings

"For the first time, I feel like I'm actually planning the surgery — not managing software."

— Lead Surgeon, Pilot Hospital Network B · Post-session debrief
01

Domain immersion is not optional in MedTech

I didn't understand what a DICOM series actually was until week 3. I didn't understand the difference between a segmentation and a mesh until week 5. Without that domain knowledge, I couldn't have evaluated whether an interaction model made clinical sense. In regulated domains, UX work requires genuine technical fluency.

02

The right abstraction is a design decision

The anatomy-first hierarchy wasn't a labeling change — it was a fundamental reframing of what the product is. Choosing what to expose and what to hide from the user is design's core power. The wrong abstraction (technical objects) made the product feel hostile. The right abstraction (anatomical structures) made it feel like an extension of clinical thinking.

03

Friction can be a feature

The named checkpoint system added a deliberate pause to the save flow. In a consumer product, I would have fought to remove it. In a clinical planning tool, it became a trust-building mechanism. The brief, intentional friction communicated seriousness and control — qualities that matter enormously in a domain where errors have physical consequences.

04

UX and engineering constraints are the same constraint

The one-click XR sync required significant server-side infrastructure. Early in the project, I designed it as a user-facing simplification and assumed the engineering would follow. In practice, I had to deeply understand the conversion pipeline to design the status model correctly. Designing the loading states and error conditions required knowing what could actually fail. UX decisions were engineering decisions and vice versa.

Personal Note

This was the most technically and ethically demanding project I've worked on. Designing for a context where your decisions contribute to patient outcomes changes how you approach every choice. I came out of it with a much more serious relationship to the craft — and a deeper respect for the surgeons who trusted us with their workflow. I'm proud of what the team built. I'm also aware that the real test isn't a usability session — it's a patient on a table. That awareness is something I carry forward.
